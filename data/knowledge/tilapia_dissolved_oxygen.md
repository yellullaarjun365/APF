# Nile Tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) -- Dissolved Oxygen in Detail

Paraphrased from published aquaculture research, including Tran-Duy, van Dam & Schrama (2012, Aquaculture Research), Elnady, Abd El Wahed & Abduljabbar (2011, Bull. Fac. Agric. Cairo Univ.), and hypoxia-physiology work on Oreochromis niloticus.

## Why dissolved oxygen (DO) matters most at night

Dissolved oxygen is widely regarded as the single most limiting water-quality factor in pond aquaculture. During the day, phytoplankton and algae in the pond photosynthesize and add oxygen to the water. At night, that process stops, but fish, plankton, and bacteria keep consuming oxygen -- so DO steadily drops overnight and reaches its lowest point in the pre-dawn hours, right before sunrise. This is the highest-risk window for oxygen-related stress and mortality, and it is why regular monitoring (not just daytime spot-checks) matters.

## Practical thresholds

- A commonly used general safety threshold for warm-water pond fish, including tilapia, is to keep DO above roughly 3 mg/L; concentrations below this are associated with reduced growth and increased mortality risk.
- Feed intake research on Nile tilapia found the point where feed intake starts to level off (the "incipient" DO) differs by fish size: for smaller fish (under 100 g) feed intake plateaus around 3 mg/L DO, while larger fish (over 200 g) keep increasing feed intake up to roughly 5.5 mg/L DO before leveling off. In other words, larger fish are somewhat more sensitive to moderate oxygen reduction in terms of appetite, even though tilapia as a species is comparatively hypoxia-tolerant.
- Nile tilapia is notably more tolerant of low oxygen than many other farmed fish species. Physiological studies show tilapia can survive extended periods of severe hypoxia by depressing their metabolic rate, and they do not lose equilibrium until oxygen levels are near-anoxic (almost zero) -- this hardiness is part of why the species is so widely farmed. This tolerance is a safety margin, not a reason to ignore DO management, since growth and feed efficiency still suffer well before survival is threatened.

## Practical takeaway for farmers

- Aim to keep DO reliably above 4-5 mg/L, especially overnight and at dawn, for healthy growth rather than just survival.
- Higher stocking density, higher feed loads, and warmer water all increase oxygen demand and lower how much oxygen the water can hold, compounding overnight DO drops -- these factors should be considered together, not separately.
- Aeration (mechanical aerators, paddle wheels, or similar) is the standard practical response when DO risk is high, particularly for intensively stocked ponds.
