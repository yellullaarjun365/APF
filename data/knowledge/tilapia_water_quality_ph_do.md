# Nile Tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) -- Water Quality: pH and Dissolved Oxygen

Source material paraphrased from published aquaculture research on Nile tilapia water-quality tolerance, primarily Mustapha & Atolagbe (2018, Journal of Basic and Applied Zoology) and Rebouças et al. (2016, Acta Scientiarum: Animal Sciences).

## Why pH matters

Nile tilapia is generally tolerant of a fairly wide pH range compared to many farmed fish species, but pH still affects survival, growth, and stress levels, especially at the extremes. Sharp or sudden pH swings are more harmful than a stable pH that sits slightly outside the ideal range, because sudden changes cause metabolic stress and can damage gill tissue.

## Optimal and tolerable ranges

Different studies report slightly different figures, reflecting real variation across life stages and rearing conditions:
- A commonly cited optimal range for general Nile tilapia culture is pH 7 to 8.
- Research specifically on juveniles reared in eutrophic (nutrient-rich) water found tilapia grow well across a noticeably wider band, with a suitable culture range assessed at roughly pH 5.5 to 9.0, since acidification up to about pH 5.5 did not significantly harm final body weight, growth rate, or yield in that study.
- Survival drops sharply at strongly acidic extremes: in acute exposure trials, fingerling survival was very poor at pH 3 and pH 4 (near-total mortality within 14 days), improved somewhat at pH 5 and pH 6, and was highest at pH 8. Juveniles and adults tolerated low pH better than fingerlings, indicating younger life stages are more vulnerable to acidic stress.

## Practical takeaway for farmers

- Keep pH as stable as possible; avoid rapid swings even within an otherwise acceptable range.
- A pH roughly between 6.5 and 8.5 is a safe practical target for most ponds.
- Fingerlings and newly stocked fish are more sensitive to low pH than older juveniles or adults -- extra care with acclimatization matters most at stocking time.
- Very acidic water (pH below about 5) sharply increases mortality risk, especially for young fish.

## Dissolved oxygen (brief note)

Low dissolved oxygen (DO) is one of the most common causes of stress and mass mortality in tilapia ponds, particularly overnight and in early morning when photosynthesis has stopped and pond organisms are still consuming oxygen. Maintaining DO above roughly 4-5 mg/L is a widely used practical safety threshold in tilapia farming; levels below that raise the risk of stress, reduced feeding, and mortality, especially when combined with high stocking density or high water temperature (which itself lowers water's oxygen-holding capacity).
