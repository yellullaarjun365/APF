# Freshwater Fish Beyond Tilapia

Largemouth bass (Micropterus salmoides) is one of the most popular freshwater sport fish in North America, an ambush predator found in warm, weedy lakes, ponds, and slow rivers. It is not typically farmed for food at commercial scale (unlike tilapia or catfish) but supports a massive recreational fishing and tournament industry, and is stocked extensively in managed lakes and ponds specifically for angling rather than harvest.

Rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) is farmed extensively in cooler climates unsuitable for tilapia, using raceways (long, narrow flowing-water channels) or cold-water ponds, since trout require well-oxygenated water below about 21°C and suffer stress or death in warmer conditions. Trout farming is a major industry in the United States (particularly Idaho), Chile, and parts of Europe, and unlike tilapia, wild and sea-run trout populations are also significant for recreational and commercial wild fisheries.

Common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is one of the most widely distributed freshwater fish in the world, native to Europe and Asia but introduced almost everywhere else, sometimes becoming a damaging invasive species (as in parts of North America and Australia) where it uproots aquatic vegetation and increases water turbidity while feeding. Despite its invasive reputation in some regions, carp remains a major food fish in its native and introduced Asian farming range.

Northern pike (Esox lucius) is an ambush predator found in cool freshwater lakes and rivers across the Northern Hemisphere, recognizable by its elongated body and duck-bill-shaped jaw lined with sharp teeth. Pike are almost exclusively managed as a wild and recreational sport fishery rather than farmed, since their solitary, cannibalistic, highly predatory nature makes dense aquaculture stocking impractical.

Catla (Catla catla) and rohu (Labeo rohita) are major carp species native to South Asian river systems and are staple farmed fish across India, Bangladesh, and neighboring countries, often raised in polyculture alongside other carp species in a similar niche-partitioning approach used with Chinese carps. Together with other Indian major carps, they represent a substantial share of South Asia's total freshwater aquaculture production.

Freshwater fish farming faces a different set of water-quality priorities than marine or brackish aquaculture: ammonia and nitrite toxicity thresholds differ because freshwater fish lack the same osmoregulatory buffering that some brackish/marine species have, and temperature tolerance windows vary drastically between cold-water species like trout and warm-water species like tilapia and carp, making species selection highly dependent on local climate and available water temperature.
