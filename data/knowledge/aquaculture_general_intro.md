# General Introduction to Aquaculture

Paraphrased from standard aquaculture reference/training material, including FAO's definition of aquaculture and general agricultural-extension training resources on fish farming.

## What aquaculture is

Aquaculture is the farming of aquatic organisms -- fish, crustaceans (like shrimp), molluscs, and aquatic plants -- under controlled or semi-controlled conditions, as opposed to catching them from the wild. The defining feature is human intervention in the life cycle: activities like stocking, feeding, protecting from predators, and controlled harvesting distinguish aquaculture from wild capture fisheries. It can take place in freshwater, brackish water, or full seawater environments.

## Main types of aquaculture systems

- **Pond culture**: the most common form for tilapia and many freshwater species -- fish are raised in earthen or lined ponds, relying partly on the pond's natural productivity (algae, plankton) and partly on supplemental feed.
- **Cage or pen culture**: fish are raised in net enclosures placed within a larger natural water body (a lake, reservoir, or river), letting water exchange freely while containing the stock.
- **Recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS)**: a closed, tank-based system where water is continuously filtered and reused, giving tight control over water quality at higher infrastructure cost.
- **Integrated systems**: such as rice-fish culture (raising fish within flooded rice paddies) or aquaponics (combining fish farming with growing plants that use the fish waste as nutrients).

## Intensity levels

Aquaculture operations are often described by how much external input (feed, aeration, water exchange) they rely on:
- **Extensive**: low stocking density, minimal external feed, relies mostly on the water body's natural food supply. Low cost and low yield per unit area.
- **Semi-intensive**: moderate stocking density with some supplemental feeding and pond management (fertilization, partial aeration).
- **Intensive**: high stocking density, complete formulated feed, and active water-quality management (aeration, water exchange) -- higher yield per unit area but higher cost, infrastructure, and management demands, and higher risk if water quality is not actively managed.

## Why it matters economically

Aquaculture has grown into one of the fastest-growing food-production sectors globally, helping meet seafood and protein demand that wild capture fisheries alone cannot sustain. For smallholder farmers, fish farming offers a way to diversify income and land use without requiring large landholdings, and is practiced by a wide range of farmers regardless of gender or landholding size.
