# Nile Tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) -- Ammonia and Nitrite Toxicity

Paraphrased from published aquaculture and toxicology research, including work summarized by Hallikainen's tilapia aquaculture technical reference pages and toxicology studies on Oreochromis niloticus (e.g. nitrite LC50 studies, ammonia stress studies).

## Where ammonia comes from

Ammonia is produced naturally as a waste product when fish excrete nitrogen from digested protein. In intensively stocked ponds, or ponds with limited water exchange or biological filtration, ammonia can build up to harmful levels. Even though tilapia are considered a relatively hardy species, they are still susceptible to ammonia poisoning under these conditions.

## Ionized vs. un-ionized ammonia -- why pH matters here too

Ammonia in water exists in two forms that are constantly interconverting: the charged, ionized form (NH4+) and the uncharged, un-ionized form (NH3). The un-ionized form is far more toxic because it can pass through cell membranes; the ionized form largely cannot. The balance between the two forms shifts with pH: as water becomes more alkaline (higher pH), a larger share of total ammonia converts to the more toxic un-ionized form. This means the same total ammonia reading can be more or less dangerous depending on the pond's pH -- high pH and high ammonia together are a particularly risky combination.

## Nitrite

Nitrite is an intermediate product formed when beneficial bacteria convert ammonia toward nitrate (a much less toxic end product), a process called nitrification. If nitrification is incomplete or bacterial populations are insufficient, nitrite itself can accumulate and become toxic. Nitrite poisoning works by binding to the fish's blood, converting functional hemoglobin into methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen -- effectively suffocating the fish internally even when there is enough dissolved oxygen in the water. Laboratory toxicity trials found that lethal nitrite concentrations for tilapia increase (i.e. nitrite becomes less dangerous) when water chloride levels are higher, because chloride competes with nitrite for the same uptake pathway in the fish's gills. This is why chloride or salt is sometimes added as a practical countermeasure in ponds with known nitrite problems.

## Practical takeaway for farmers

- Test ammonia and nitrite periodically, not just when fish already show signs of distress -- both can build up gradually and are not always visible.
- If pH is already on the higher side of the acceptable range, be extra cautious about ammonia buildup, since more of it will be in the toxic un-ionized form.
- Signs of ammonia or nitrite stress include reduced feeding, lethargy, and gasping at the surface; these overlap with low-DO symptoms, so ruling out both is good practice when fish appear stressed.
- Regular partial water exchange, avoiding overfeeding (uneaten feed decays into ammonia), and not overstocking beyond what the pond's biological filtration can handle are the standard practical controls.
