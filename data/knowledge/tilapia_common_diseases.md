# Nile Tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) -- Common Disease Awareness

Paraphrased from published aquaculture health literature, including FAO bacterial-disease reference material, tilapia parasite-disease reviews, and regional farm-survey studies (e.g. Uganda, Egypt, Mexico, Philippines). This file is for general awareness only -- it is not a diagnostic or treatment guide, and any suspected disease outbreak should be assessed by a qualified fish health professional or local extension service, since treatment depends on correct identification and local regulations.

## Why disease risk rises with intensification

Across multiple regional surveys, disease outbreaks in tilapia farming become more frequent and more severe as culture systems are intensified -- higher stocking density, more feed input, and less water exchange all increase stress on fish and create conditions where bacteria and parasites spread more easily. Poor water quality (low dissolved oxygen, high ammonia, overcrowding) is repeatedly identified as a contributing factor alongside the pathogens themselves, not a separate issue.

## Common bacterial diseases

- **Streptococcosis** (caused by Streptococcus agalactiae or related species) is one of the most economically damaging bacterial diseases in farmed tilapia worldwide. Reported clinical signs include erratic or abnormal swimming, bulging eyes (exophthalmia), darkening of the skin, loss of appetite, and a swollen abdomen. It can cause significant biomass and economic loss during outbreaks.
- **Columnaris disease** (caused by Flavobacterium columnare, formerly Flexibacter columnaris) commonly shows as fin and tail rot, gill congestion, skin ulceration, and a characteristic thick yellowish mucus on affected skin and gills. It is strongly associated with overstocked ponds and poor husbandry conditions, and can affect fish through injured gills as well as, at high water temperatures, apparently healthy gill tissue.
- Other bacterial pathogens reported across regional surveys include various Aeromonas, Edwardsiella, Pseudomonas, and Vibrio species, generally linked to similar stress and water-quality risk factors.

## Common parasites

Parasitic problems tend to increase with culture intensification as well. Commonly reported parasites in tilapia farming include:
- **Trichodina** (a ciliate) -- associated with erratic swimming, fish scraping against pond walls, and fin/skin erosion; can cause serious losses in hatchery and nursery stages.
- **Ichthyophthirius multifiliis** ("Ich") -- causes visible white spots on the skin, thick skin mucus, and stunted growth; most severe in larval/juvenile stages.
- Various trematodes and other protozoans are also reported in regional surveys, generally tied to water-quality and biosecurity conditions rather than being unavoidable.

## Practical takeaway for farmers

- Good water-quality management (adequate DO, controlled ammonia, appropriate stocking density) is the single most consistently cited preventive factor across the disease literature -- prevention through water quality is far more reliable than treating an active outbreak.
- Warning signs worth acting on quickly include: fish gathering at the surface or gasping, erratic swimming, loss of appetite, visible skin/fin damage, unusual dark coloration, or bulging eyes -- these overlap across several diseases and water-quality problems, so a sudden change in fish behavior is itself the signal to investigate, not any single symptom.
- If disease is suspected, isolating or reporting to a local aquaculture extension office or fish health professional for proper diagnosis is the recommended next step rather than guessing at treatment.
