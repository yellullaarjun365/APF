# Nile Tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) -- Feed Conversion Ratio, Growth Rate, and Harvest Economics

Paraphrased from published aquaculture economics and growth-performance research, including Omasaki et al. (2017, Aquaculture) on economic values of production traits, and pond growth-performance trials (e.g. Bahnasawy et al., 2003, Archives of Polish Fisheries).

## What Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) means

FCR is the amount of feed consumed divided by the weight gained by the fish -- lower FCR means more efficient conversion of feed into fish biomass, and is one of the most direct drivers of farm profitability since feed is typically the largest single production cost. In one earthen-pond growth trial using supplemental feeding, an FCR of roughly 0.82 was achieved under favorable fertilized-pond conditions with natural food supplementing the formulated feed; in other studies using purely formulated feed without pond fertilization, FCR values in the 1.0-1.7 range were more typical, with lower values considered better performance.

## What most strongly affects economic returns

An economic-value analysis of Nile tilapia production traits found that growth rate (specifically, the fish's thermal growth coefficient) had a substantially larger effect on farm gross margin than feed conversion ratio alone. This is because faster growth shortens the grow-out period, which lets a farm complete more production cycles per year and reduces both the total feed used and the oxygen demand per unit of fish produced over time -- the benefit compounds through the whole production cycle, not just at harvest. In practical terms, this means culture duration (how many days to reach harvest weight) is not just a scheduling detail -- it is one of the biggest economic levers a farmer has, alongside FCR itself.

## Feeding rate and frequency effects

Research on feeding strategy found that feeding fish on alternate days or at half-ration (rather than full ration every day) can actually produce a *better* FCR than full daily feeding, because it reduces wasted/uneaten feed while causing no significant loss in growth performance -- in one Ghana-based trial, alternate-day feeding produced the highest profit of the strategies tested. Separately, increasing feeding frequency (more, smaller feedings per day rather than fewer large ones) has also been shown to improve feed conversion and reduce total feed needed per ton of fish produced in cage culture. Both findings point to the same underlying principle: matching feed delivery to what fish can actually use, rather than maximizing total feed given, tends to improve both growth efficiency and cost.

## Practical takeaway for farmers

- FCR and culture duration together are the two biggest economic levers in tilapia production -- reducing either directly improves gross margin.
- Overfeeding does not reliably speed up growth and can actually worsen FCR and water quality (excess feed decays into ammonia); matching feeding rate/frequency to what fish are actually consuming is usually more profitable than simply feeding more.
- Market price also varies with harvested fish size in some studies, so target harvest weight -- not just total biomass -- can matter for revenue, depending on local market preferences.
