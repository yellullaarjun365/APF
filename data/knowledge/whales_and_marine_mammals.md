# Whales and Marine Mammals -- Overview, with Blue Whale Detail

Paraphrased from general cetacean-biology and conservation reference material (e.g. National Geographic Kids, IUCN status summaries, wildlife agency fact sheets).

## Marine mammal groups

- **Cetaceans**: whales, dolphins, and porpoises. Divided into baleen whales (which filter-feed using baleen plates instead of teeth -- includes blue whales, humpback whales, and gray whales) and toothed whales (which hunt prey individually -- includes sperm whales, orcas, and all dolphins/porpoises).
- **Pinnipeds**: seals, sea lions, and walruses -- semi-aquatic, spending time both in water and on land/ice.
- **Sirenians**: manatees and dugongs -- slow-moving, plant-eating marine mammals found in warm coastal waters and rivers.

## Blue whale (Balaenoptera musculus)

- **Size**: the largest animal known to have ever existed on Earth, larger than any dinosaur. Adults typically reach 24-30 meters (80-100 feet) in length, with the largest recorded individuals up to about 33 meters; weight is commonly cited around 130,000-200,000 kg (roughly 130-200 metric tons). Females are generally larger than males.
- **Diet**: blue whales feed almost exclusively on krill, small shrimp-like crustaceans, despite their enormous size. They are filter feeders -- lacking teeth, they take in huge amounts of water and prey, then filter it through baleen plates in their mouth, straining out the krill. A single blue whale can consume several tons of krill per day during feeding season.
- **Anatomy notes often cited for scale**: a blue whale's tongue can weigh as much as an elephant, and its heart is roughly the size of a small car -- large enough that a small child could reportedly crawl through its major blood vessels.
- **Habitat and range**: found in all major oceans except the Arctic. They are generally solitary or found in small groups, and migrate long distances between cold-water feeding grounds (rich in krill) and warmer breeding grounds.
- **Lifespan**: estimated at 80-90 years in the wild, with some individuals living over 100 years.
- **Conservation status**: listed as Endangered by the IUCN. Blue whale populations were severely reduced by 20th-century commercial whaling and remain threatened today by ship strikes, entanglement in fishing gear, ocean noise, and climate change effects on krill populations.

## Why this matters for general awareness

Blue whales are a commonly referenced example of extreme scale in nature, and their conservation status reflects a broader pattern seen across many large marine mammal species: slow reproduction rates and historical overexploitation make population recovery a multi-generational process even after protective measures are put in place.
