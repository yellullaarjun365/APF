# Climate Change and Ocean Impacts

Ocean warming has raised average global sea surface temperature measurably over the past century, and the ocean has absorbed over 90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases since the mid-20th century, making the ocean the planet's dominant heat sink. This absorbed heat drives coral bleaching events, shifts species distributions toward cooler waters (typically poleward or to greater depths), and disrupts the timing of seasonal events like spawning and migration that many marine species depend on.

Marine heatwaves -- prolonged periods of anomalously high ocean temperature in a specific region -- have become more frequent and intense in recent decades, with notable examples including the "Blob" in the North Pacific (2014-2016), which caused mass die-offs of seabirds and marine mammals and closed commercial fisheries due to toxic algal blooms thriving in the warmer water. These events illustrate how warming affects marine ecosystems in sudden, acute pulses rather than only as a slow gradual trend.

Sea level rise, driven both by thermal expansion of warming seawater and melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets), threatens coastal aquaculture infrastructure directly -- ponds, hatcheries, and processing facilities in low-lying coastal zones face increased flooding risk and saltwater intrusion into what were previously freshwater systems, which can be catastrophic for freshwater aquaculture species like tilapia if pond salinity rises beyond their tolerance.

Changing precipitation patterns tied to climate change affect freshwater aquaculture both through drought (reducing water availability for pond filling and exchange) and through more intense rainfall events (risking pond overflow, dilution of water quality parameters, and infrastructure damage). Farmers in regions experiencing more erratic rainfall increasingly rely on groundwater or stored water reserves as a buffer, adding cost and complexity to what was previously rain-fed pond management.

Species range shifts are among the most well-documented ocean impacts of warming: many commercially important fish species are moving toward the poles or into deeper, cooler water as their historical ranges warm beyond their thermal tolerance, disrupting fisheries that were built around historical species distributions and creating new international disputes over fishing rights as species cross national maritime boundaries into new territory.

Aquaculture is sometimes framed as part of climate adaptation for global food security, since it can be sited and managed with more direct control over water conditions than wild-capture fisheries (which are fully exposed to ocean-wide climate shifts), though land-based and pond aquaculture still face their own climate exposure through temperature, water availability, and extreme weather risk to physical infrastructure.
