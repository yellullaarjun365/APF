# Marine Conservation and Endangered Species

The IUCN Red List classifies species by extinction risk into categories including Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, and Extinct. Many well-known marine species carry threatened classifications: the vaquita porpoise (fewer than 20 individuals remaining, the world's most endangered marine mammal), the North Atlantic right whale (fewer than 400 remaining, threatened mainly by ship strikes and fishing gear entanglement), and hawksbill sea turtles (Critically Endangered, historically hunted for their shells).

Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) are designated ocean regions where human activity is restricted or regulated to conserve marine ecosystems, ranging from fully no-take zones where all extraction is banned to multi-use areas that allow limited, regulated fishing. Well-enforced no-take MPAs have been shown to increase fish biomass inside their boundaries within years, with a documented "spillover effect" where increased fish populations move beyond the protected area and benefit adjacent fisheries.

Ship strikes are a leading cause of death for large whale species, particularly in busy shipping lanes that overlap with migration routes, such as those used by North Atlantic right whales along the U.S. East Coast. Mitigation measures include seasonal speed restrictions for vessels in known whale habitat, rerouting shipping lanes away from critical areas, and real-time whale-detection alert systems for ship captains.

Entanglement in fishing gear (ghost nets, lobster and crab pot lines, gillnets) kills or injures large numbers of marine mammals and sea turtles annually. Whales can drag gear for months, leading to exhaustion, infection, and impaired feeding; efforts to reduce entanglement include ropeless fishing gear that eliminates vertical lines in the water column and stricter gear-marking requirements to trace entanglements back to specific fisheries.

Ocean acidification, caused by the ocean absorbing roughly a quarter of human-emitted carbon dioxide, lowers seawater pH and reduces the availability of carbonate ions that shell-building organisms (corals, oysters, some plankton) need to form their calcium carbonate structures. This is a distinct threat from ocean warming, though the two often compound each other's effects on marine ecosystems.

Sea turtle conservation has benefited significantly from protected nesting beaches, turtle excluder devices (TEDs) in shrimp trawl nets that allow turtles to escape captured nets, and international bans on trade in turtle products under CITES (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species). Several sea turtle populations have shown measurable recovery where these protections have been consistently enforced over multiple decades.
