# Fish Anatomy and Physiology

Gills are the primary respiratory organ in fish, made up of thin filaments packed with blood vessels that extract dissolved oxygen from water as it flows over them, typically in the opposite direction to blood flow (countercurrent exchange), which maximizes oxygen absorption efficiency compared to same-direction flow. This is why dissolved oxygen concentration in water is so critical to farmed fish health -- unlike air-breathing animals, fish have no alternative oxygen source if water DO drops too low.

Most bony fish (including tilapia, carp, and salmon) have a swim bladder, a gas-filled internal organ that allows the fish to control buoyancy and remain suspended at a chosen depth without constantly swimming, by adjusting the volume of gas inside it. Cartilaginous fish like sharks and rays lack a swim bladder entirely and must rely on constant swimming and an oil-rich liver for partial buoyancy, which is part of why many shark species sink and must keep moving to stay off the bottom.

Fish are ectothermic ("cold-blooded"), meaning their body temperature matches the surrounding water rather than being internally regulated, which is why water temperature so directly controls metabolic rate, feeding behavior, and growth speed in farmed species -- warmer water (within a species' tolerance range) speeds up digestion and growth, while colder water slows both. A small number of fish, like some tuna and mackerel shark species, have evolved partial endothermy in specific muscles to maintain higher-than-water temperatures for sustained fast swimming.

The lateral line is a sensory organ running along the sides of most fish, consisting of a series of fluid-filled canals with hair-cell receptors that detect changes in water pressure and movement, allowing fish to sense nearby objects, predators, prey, and even schooling neighbors without relying on sight, which is especially useful in murky water or darkness.

Scales serve as external protection and come in several distinct types across fish groups: cycloid scales (smooth-edged, found in tilapia and carp), ctenoid scales (comb-edged, found in many spiny-finned fish like bass), placoid scales (tooth-like, found in sharks and rays, giving their skin a sandpaper texture), and ganoid scales (thick, diamond-shaped, found in more primitive fish like gar). Scale loss from handling or netting can compromise a fish's protective mucus layer and increase infection risk, which is part of why gentle handling matters during harvest and transport.

Osmoregulation is the process by which fish maintain proper internal salt and water balance despite living in an external environment with a very different salinity than their body fluids. Freshwater fish constantly absorb water and must actively excrete excess water while retaining salts (mainly through gills and kidneys), while marine fish face the opposite problem -- constantly losing water to their saltier surroundings -- and must drink seawater and excrete concentrated salt. Species that move between fresh and salt water (like some tilapia strains, salmon, and eels) have specialized physiological adaptations to switch between these opposing strategies.
