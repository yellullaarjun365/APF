# Fishing Industry and Sustainability

Global wild fisheries production has plateaued since the late 1980s at roughly 90-95 million tonnes per year, even as fishing effort and technology have increased, a strong indicator that many wild stocks are being fished at or beyond their maximum sustainable yield. Aquaculture, by contrast, has grown continuously and now supplies over half of all fish consumed by humans globally, making it the fastest-growing food production sector in the world.

Overfishing occurs when fish are caught faster than populations can reproduce and replenish, and the FAO estimates that roughly a third of assessed global fish stocks are currently overfished, with the remainder split between fully fished (no room for increased catch) and underfished. Species with slow growth and late reproductive maturity, such as many sharks, tuna, and deep-sea fish, are especially vulnerable to overfishing because their populations recover slowly even after fishing pressure is reduced.

Bycatch refers to the unintentional capture of non-target species during fishing operations, including juvenile fish, sea turtles, seabirds, and marine mammals, much of which is discarded dead or dying. Bottom trawling, which drags weighted nets along the seafloor, has some of the highest bycatch rates and also causes significant physical damage to seafloor habitats like coral and sponge beds; more selective gear types (like circle hooks or turtle excluder devices) are increasingly mandated to reduce this impact.

Aquaculture, while easing pressure on wild stocks for many species, carries its own sustainability tradeoffs. Carnivorous farmed species like salmon and shrimp require fishmeal and fish oil in their feed, often sourced from wild-caught forage fish, so intensive carnivorous aquaculture can still draw down wild fish populations indirectly. This has driven research into alternative feed ingredients such as insect meal, algae-based oils, and plant proteins to reduce dependence on wild forage fish.

Certification programs like the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC) for wild-caught seafood and the Aquaculture Stewardship Council (ASC) for farmed seafood assess fisheries and farms against environmental and social standards, allowing certified products to carry a sustainability label. Critics note that certification schemes vary in rigor and that certified sustainable seafood still represents a minority of global production, but they remain one of the primary consumer-facing tools for encouraging more sustainable sourcing.

Illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing is estimated to account for up to a fifth of the global wild catch, undermining fisheries management efforts and disproportionately affecting developing coastal nations that lack the resources for enforcement and monitoring. Satellite tracking of vessel movements and international port-state agreements requiring documentation of catch origin are two of the main tools used to combat IUU fishing.
