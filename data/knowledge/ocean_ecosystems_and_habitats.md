# Ocean Ecosystems and Habitats

Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but support roughly a quarter of all marine species, earning them the nickname "rainforests of the sea." Reefs are built by colonies of tiny coral polyps that secrete calcium carbonate skeletons over centuries, forming structures that shelter fish, mollusks, and countless invertebrates. Rising ocean temperatures cause coral bleaching, where corals expel the symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) that give them color and much of their food energy, leaving them white and vulnerable to starvation and disease.

The deep sea, generally defined as waters below 200 meters where sunlight no longer penetrates, is the largest habitat on Earth by volume yet remains largely unexplored. Deep-sea organisms have adapted to extreme pressure, near-freezing temperatures, and permanent darkness -- many species are bioluminescent, producing their own light to attract prey, evade predators, or communicate. Hydrothermal vents, cracks in the ocean floor that release mineral-rich superheated water, support entire ecosystems built not on sunlight-driven photosynthesis but on chemosynthesis, where bacteria convert chemicals like hydrogen sulfide into energy.

Mangrove forests grow in intertidal zones along tropical and subtropical coastlines, with root systems that trap sediment, filter pollutants, and provide nursery habitat for juvenile fish and shrimp before they move to open water as adults. Mangroves also act as natural coastal barriers, reducing wave energy and protecting inland areas from storm surges and erosion. An estimated one-third of mangrove forests worldwide have been lost since the mid-20th century, largely due to conversion into shrimp aquaculture ponds, agriculture, and coastal development.

Kelp forests, dense underwater stands of large brown algae, thrive in cold, nutrient-rich coastal waters and are among the most productive ecosystems on the planet, growing up to half a meter per day under ideal conditions. They provide habitat and food for sea urchins, otters, fish, and invertebrates, and are themselves kept in check by predators like sea otters, which control urchin populations that would otherwise overgraze kelp into barren rock.

Estuaries, where freshwater rivers meet the sea, are among the most biologically productive ecosystems due to the constant nutrient input from land runoff mixing with marine water. Salinity in estuaries fluctuates with tides and freshwater flow, so species living there -- including many commercially important fish and shellfish -- must tolerate a wide range of salinity, a trait called euryhalinity.

Open ocean (pelagic) zones are divided by depth into distinct layers: the sunlit epipelagic zone (0-200m) where photosynthesis occurs and most marine life is concentrated; the twilight mesopelagic zone (200-1000m) with faint light and many vertically migrating species; and the aphotic zones below, entirely without sunlight, where deep-sea adaptations dominate.
