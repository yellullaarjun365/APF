# Aquaculture Species Beyond Tilapia

Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) is the most economically significant finfish in global aquaculture by value, farmed mainly in sea cages in Norway, Chile, Scotland, and Canada. Farmed salmon are raised through a two-stage cycle: freshwater hatcheries for the first 12-18 months until smoltification (the physiological change allowing survival in seawater), then open-water net pens for 12-24 months until harvest at 4-6 kg. Major challenges include sea lice infestations, escapes affecting wild populations, and high feed costs since salmon are carnivorous and require fishmeal or fish-oil-based feed.

Whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) is the dominant farmed shrimp species worldwide, prized for fast growth, disease tolerance, and adaptability to varying salinities. It is farmed in earthen ponds across Ecuador, India, Vietnam, Indonesia, and China, typically stocked at high densities in intensive systems with aeration and biofloc technology. A major disease threat is White Spot Syndrome Virus (WSSV), which can wipe out an entire pond within days, making biosecurity (filtered water intake, disinfected equipment, quarantine of new stock) essential.

Channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) is the primary aquaculture species in the United States, farmed mainly in pond systems across Mississippi, Alabama, and Arkansas. It tolerates a wide temperature range and lower dissolved oxygen than many farmed species, making it relatively resilient to pond management lapses. Catfish farming typically uses a multiple-batch cropping system, where fish of different age classes share the same pond, harvested selectively as they reach market size (roughly 0.5-1 kg).

Common carp (Cyprinus carpio) and various Chinese carp species (grass carp, silver carp, bighead carp) together form the largest volume of farmed fish globally, especially across China, India, and Eastern Europe. Polyculture -- stocking multiple carp species with complementary feeding niches (grass carp eat vegetation, silver carp filter phytoplankton, bighead carp filter zooplankton, common carp forage the bottom) in the same pond -- maximizes total yield per unit area without competing for the same food source.

Pacific white shrimp and Atlantic salmon together account for the majority of aquaculture export value, while carp species (mostly consumed domestically in Asia) account for the largest volume. This value-versus-volume distinction matters for market analysis: a country can dominate global aquaculture tonnage (like China with carp) without dominating export revenue (which favors higher-value species like salmon and shrimp).

Barramundi (Lates calcarifer), also called Asian sea bass, is an increasingly popular farmed species in Australia and Southeast Asia due to its ability to tolerate both fresh and brackish water, fast growth to market size (around 8-12 months to 500g-1kg), and mild white flesh that suits diverse cuisines. It is typically farmed in sea cages, ponds, or increasingly in land-based recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS).

Recirculating Aquaculture Systems (RAS) are an increasingly common production method across multiple species (salmon smolts, barramundi, tilapia, trout) where water is continuously filtered, oxygenated, and reused rather than exchanged with the environment. RAS allows farming in locations far from natural water bodies, tighter control over water quality parameters, and reduced disease/parasite risk from external sources, but comes with high capital and energy costs for pumping, filtration, and temperature control.
