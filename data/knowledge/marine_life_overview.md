# General Marine Life -- Overview of Life in the Ocean

General-knowledge reference material on marine biodiversity, paraphrased from standard marine-science and conservation reference sources.

## How much life is in the ocean

The ocean covers about 71% of Earth's surface and holds the vast majority of the planet's living space by volume, since life exists from the sunlit surface down to the deepest ocean trenches, over 10 kilometers deep. Marine life is organized loosely by depth zone: the sunlit "epipelagic" zone near the surface (where most fish, corals, and plant-like plankton live), the dim "mesopelagic" zone, and the pitch-black deep sea, where organisms rely on bioluminescence, chemosynthesis (using chemical energy instead of sunlight), or scavenging.

## Major groups of marine life

- **Fish**: the most diverse group of marine vertebrates, ranging from tiny reef fish to large predators like sharks and tuna. Cartilaginous fish (sharks, rays) and bony fish (the vast majority of species) are the two main groups.
- **Marine mammals**: whales, dolphins, porpoises (cetaceans), seals and sea lions (pinnipeds), and manatees/dugongs (sirenians) -- all air-breathing, warm-blooded animals that returned to the ocean from land-dwelling ancestors over evolutionary time.
- **Invertebrates**: an enormous and diverse category including crustaceans (crabs, shrimp, lobster), molluscs (octopus, squid, clams, oysters), echinoderms (starfish, sea urchins), and cnidarians (jellyfish, corals, anemones).
- **Plankton**: drifting organisms, both plant-like (phytoplankton, which produce a large share of the world's oxygen through photosynthesis) and animal-like (zooplankton), forming the base of nearly all marine food webs.

## Coral reefs

Coral reefs occupy less than 1% of the ocean floor but are estimated to support roughly a quarter of all known marine species, making them one of the most biodiverse ecosystems on Earth per unit area, often compared to rainforests in this respect. Reefs are built by colonies of tiny coral polyps that secrete calcium carbonate skeletons over long timescales.

## Why ocean biodiversity matters

Marine ecosystems regulate global climate (the ocean absorbs a large share of human-generated CO2 and heat), support fisheries that feed billions of people worldwide, and remain only partially explored -- scientists estimate a large fraction of ocean species, especially in the deep sea, have not yet been formally identified or described.
