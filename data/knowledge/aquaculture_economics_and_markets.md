# Aquaculture Economics and Markets

Feed typically represents 50-70% of total operating costs in intensive aquaculture, making feed conversion ratio (FCR) -- the amount of feed needed to produce one unit of weight gain -- the single biggest lever for farm profitability. A tilapia farm improving its FCR from 2.0 to 1.6 (needing 1.6 kg of feed per kg of fish gained instead of 2.0 kg) can substantially cut costs without changing stocking density, culture duration, or selling price.

Market size at harvest significantly affects price per kilogram in most farmed fish markets: many buyers pay a premium for fish above a minimum size threshold (since processing costs per kg are lower for larger fish), while undersized fish are often sold at a discount or rejected outright by processors. This creates an economic incentive to extend culture duration slightly if growth rates support reaching a larger, higher-value size bracket, even though this adds feed cost and pond-occupancy time.

Farmgate price (what a farmer receives before any processing or distribution markup) for aquaculture products fluctuates with seasonal supply gluts, feed input costs (which are themselves tied to global soybean and fishmeal commodity prices), and disease outbreaks that reduce regional supply. Farmers who can time harvests to avoid regional supply gluts, or who have contracts with processors that lock in price ahead of harvest, reduce their exposure to this price volatility.

Small-scale and smallholder aquaculture, common across South and Southeast Asia and much of Africa, often operates with lower capital investment and simpler infrastructure (earthen ponds, minimal aeration, feed sourced from farm byproducts) than commercial-scale operations, trading lower yield per hectare for lower financial risk and capital requirements. Access to affordable credit is frequently cited as the biggest barrier preventing smallholder farmers from adopting yield-improving technology like aerators or higher-quality feed.

Export-oriented aquaculture species (shrimp, salmon, high-value finfish) generate substantially more revenue per tonne than staple domestic-consumption species (tilapia, carp) in most markets, but require meeting import-country food safety and traceability standards, cold-chain logistics, and often certification (like ASC or BAP) that adds cost and complexity smallholders may struggle to meet without cooperative or government support.

Insurance products for aquaculture remain underdeveloped in many regions compared to crop agriculture, partly because pond-level risk (disease outbreak, extreme weather, water quality collapse) is harder to verify and assess remotely than crop damage, making many farmers fully exposed to catastrophic loss from a single bad production cycle. This is part of the broader motivation for forecasting tools like this one -- better prediction of expected harvest outcomes can support more informed planning, financing conversations, and risk management even without a formal insurance product.
